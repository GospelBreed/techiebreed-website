A customer sends a screenshot. "I've paid, please send the order." Your sales rep looks at the screenshot, then at the phone, then calls whoever has the bank app. Someone scrolls through the day's credits looking for the right amount and a name that roughly matches. Five minutes later, or an hour later on a busy day, the order goes out. Sometimes the money is there. Sometimes it isn't.

Bank transfer is how a huge share of Nigerian customers prefer to pay, and for good reason: it's familiar, fast and works from any banking app or USSD code. The problem isn't the payment method. It's that most businesses still confirm transfers by hand, one by one, and that creates three problems at once: slow service, staff time lost to checking, and real exposure to fake alerts.

This guide shows you how to confirm bank transfer payments automatically, so your system knows a payment has landed, which order it belongs to and whether the amount is right, without anyone reading SMS alerts or bank statements. It's written for owners and operations managers. Where a developer or a no-code tool comes in, we say so.

## Why manual transfer confirmation keeps going wrong

The usual process looks like this: the customer pays into your main business account, sends proof, and someone checks the bank app. Every step has a weak point.

**Screenshots and SMS alerts can be faked.** In a [June 2025 ThisDay report on fake bank alerts](https://www.thisdaylive.com/2025/06/28/how-not-to-fall-prey-to-fake-bank-alerts), a security consultant warned that many vendors rely on receipts sent by buyers to confirm payment, and that those receipts "are increasingly being manipulated". The same report describes a POS operator who lost ₦50,000 after accepting an SMS from a "regular customer". Banks have been saying the same thing. [Legit.ng reported in 2025](https://www.legit.ng/business-economy/money/1636995-fake-alerts-banks-warn-customers-beware-give-tips-recognize-fake-alerts) that Access Bank warned customers not to rush to deliver goods after receiving an SMS or email notification, and to confirm funds through secure channels such as USSD, internet banking or its app.

**One account number for every customer makes matching guesswork.** When fifty people pay into the same account in a day, you're matching by amount and sender name. Two customers pay ₦25,000 within ten minutes. Someone pays from their sister's account. A company pays under its registered name, not the trading name your rep knows. Each of these needs a person to work out what happened.

**Confirmation depends on whoever has the bank app.** If the owner or the accountant is the only person with access, every order waits for them. That's a bottleneck, and it also means sharing banking access more widely than you should.

**Reconciliation happens days later, if at all.** Without a reliable link between each transfer and each order, the month-end books turn into an archaeology project.

None of this is unusual. Moniepoint's Monnify team, writing in [a 2021 post on the origins of "pay via bank transfer"](https://moniepoint.com/blog/going-beyond-cards-the-genesis-of-pay-via-bank-transfer), describes an insurance customer in 2018 whose transfers had to be checked by hand: someone looked to see whether the funds had entered the company's account, then confirmed that the sender's name matched the customer's. That manual check, they wrote, was why transfers were never a popular way to pay online at the time.

## What "automatic confirmation" actually means

An automated setup replaces the human check with three things:

1. **A unique way to pay for each customer or order.** Instead of your main account number, the customer pays into an account number that belongs only to them, or only to that transaction.
2. **A notification from the payment provider, not the customer.** When the money genuinely arrives, the provider sends your system a message (a *webhook*) that says who paid, how much and when. A screenshot can be faked. A webhook signed by your provider, checked by your system, is far harder to fake.
3. **An action your system takes when it hears that message.** Mark the order paid, tell the customer, alert the dispatch team, and record it for your accounts.

The rest of this guide walks through how to set each part up.

## Step 1: Decide which kind of account number you need

Nigerian payment providers offer two broad options. They solve slightly different problems, and many businesses end up using both.

### Temporary accounts for one-off payments

A temporary (or "dynamic") account is created for a single transaction and expires afterwards. It suits shops, restaurants, event tickets and one-off service payments, where each customer pays once for a specific amount.

- **Paystack "Pay with Transfer".** According to [Paystack's developer documentation](https://paystack.com/docs/v2/payments/channels/pwt), Pay with Transfer "creates a temporary account for a single transaction", and the account becomes invalid once the transaction completes or its expiry window lapses. Through the API you can set the expiry from 15 minutes up to 8 hours. On Paystack's own checkout, [its support article](https://support.paystack.com/en/articles/2128642) (edited September 2026) says generated account numbers expire after 30 minutes, and that transfers made after expiry, or for the wrong amount, are refunded to the customer automatically.
- **Flutterwave dynamic virtual accounts.** [Flutterwave's NGN virtual account documentation](https://developer.flutterwave.com/v3.0/docs/ngn-virtual-accounts) says a dynamic account needs an amount and the customer's email, and customers are expected to pay before its expiry date. It also says that if a customer pays less or more than the amount, the transfer is automatically reversed (with an exception for one bank, where the transaction fails instead).

The big advantage of a temporary account is that wrong amounts don't get through. If the customer underpays, the money goes back and the order stays unpaid, so nobody has to chase a ₦2,000 shortfall.

### Permanent accounts for repeat customers

A dedicated (or "static") virtual account is assigned to one customer and reused. It suits schools collecting fees, landlords collecting rent, wholesalers with trade customers, savings and subscription businesses, and anyone who invoices the same people again and again.

- **Paystack Dedicated Virtual Accounts.** [Paystack's documentation](https://paystack.com/docs/payments/dedicated-virtual-accounts/) says all bank transfers to a customer's dedicated account "will automatically be recorded as transactions from that customer". There are conditions: the feature is only for registered businesses in Nigeria and Ghana that have completed Paystack's go-live process, accounts should be created only with a customer's express consent, and businesses in some categories (betting, financial services and general services) must validate the customer's identity before an account number is issued.
- **Flutterwave static virtual accounts.** Flutterwave's documentation says you need the customer's NIN or BVN to create one.

Other providers, including several banks and payment service banks, offer similar virtual account products. Before choosing, check fees on the provider's current pricing page, settlement time to your main account, which banks issue the account numbers, and whether the provider can send you webhooks.

If you've already read our guide to [automating payment reminders](/insights/automate-payment-reminders-nigeria), dedicated accounts will look familiar: putting a customer's own account number in every reminder is what makes the reminders stop automatically when they pay.

## Step 2: Let the provider tell your system when money arrives

This is the step that actually ends the screenshot problem. Your payment provider sends a webhook to a web address your system controls whenever a payment lands.

- On Paystack, a successful bank transfer into a dedicated account triggers a `charge.success` event, with the payment channel shown as `dedicated_nuban` and details including the sender's name and bank. Paystack's documentation is clear that "the only way to know when a payment has been done is through webhooks", and that it notifies you only after the transfer is complete, which typically takes a few minutes.
- On Flutterwave, a completed bank transfer triggers a `charge.completed` event. Flutterwave says to store the session reference it returns (`flw_ref`) alongside your own transaction record, because that's the value you use to reconcile.

There are three security rules to hand to whoever builds this, whether that's a developer or someone configuring a no-code tool:

1. **Check the signature on every webhook.** Paystack's [webhook documentation](https://paystack.com/docs/payments/webhooks) says every event carries an `x-paystack-signature` header, a signature made with your secret key, and that verifying it "should be done before processing the event". Paystack also lists IP whitelisting as a second safeguard. An endpoint that accepts unverified messages can be fooled by anyone who finds its address.
2. **Don't trust the customer's browser or phone.** Paystack recommends using webhooks rather than callbacks to give value, because callbacks can fail if the customer's network is weak or their device switches off. The same logic applies even more strongly to screenshots.
3. **Never deliver twice.** Providers retry webhooks if your system doesn't respond quickly, so the same payment can arrive more than once. Paystack's [verify payments guide](https://paystack.com/docs/payments/verify-payments/) specifically advises businesses selling digital value to check they haven't already delivered for a transaction, to avoid double fulfilment. Record each payment reference once and ignore repeats.

As a backstop, run a scheduled check. Paystack's Verify Transaction endpoint lets your server confirm a transaction's status by its reference, and its dedicated account "requery" endpoint can prompt a check for transfers that haven't been processed yet (no more than once every ten minutes per account). A short daily or hourly job that catches anything a webhook missed keeps your records honest.

## Step 3: Decide what happens the moment a payment is confirmed

Confirmation is only useful if it triggers the next step without anyone pressing a button. Write down, for each type of sale, what should happen when the webhook arrives. A typical flow:

1. **Match.** Find the order or invoice linked to that account number or transaction reference.
2. **Check the amount.** Compare what arrived with what was due. With temporary accounts the provider usually rejects wrong amounts for you. With dedicated accounts, part-payments and overpayments can arrive, so decide your rule in advance (for example, mark as part-paid and alert a named person).
3. **Update the record.** Mark the order paid in your spreadsheet, CRM, POS or order system, with the payment reference and time.
4. **Tell the customer.** Send a short confirmation by WhatsApp, SMS or email. Our guide to [automating WhatsApp order confirmations](/insights/whatsapp-order-confirmation-automation-nigeria) covers Meta's template rules for this kind of message.
5. **Tell the team.** Alert dispatch, the kitchen, the front desk or the account manager that the order is cleared to go.
6. **Record it for accounts.** Push the transaction to your accounting tool or a daily summary sheet.

This is where the time saving really shows up. Nobody asks "has this person paid?" because the answer is already on the order.

## Step 4: Train your team on the new rule

Technology closes the gap only if people stop using the old shortcut. Agree one simple rule and put it in writing: **nothing is released until the order shows as paid in the system.** Not a screenshot, not an SMS, not "I've sent it, check again".

This protects your staff as much as your business. A rep under pressure from an insistent customer can point to the rule instead of making a judgement call. ThisDay's report quotes the same advice from a security professional: never release anything until you confirm the payment, and be wary of customers who insist on immediate confirmation.

For the few payments that still arrive outside the system, such as a customer who pays into your main account by mistake, name one person who checks and records them through your bank's official app or internet banking, and route those orders to that person.

## Step 5: Reconcile automatically, not at month end

Once every payment arrives with a reference, reconciliation becomes a report rather than a project. Each day your system should be able to show:

- total received by transfer, by channel and by account
- orders paid, part-paid and unpaid
- any payments that couldn't be matched to an order
- payouts from your provider to your main bank account, so you can match settlements to your bank statement

Larger organisations take this further. A [published case study by the reconciliation software company SolveXia](https://www.solvexia.com/case-study/gomoney) describes a Nigerian digital bank (not named on the page) that automated matching of its inward and outward transactions against NIBSS settlement files. SolveXia reports that the process reconciles over 8,000 transactions a day and saves more than 75 hours of manual effort a month. Those are the vendor's figures, for a bank-scale operation, but the principle is the same at any size: match automatically, and let people handle only the exceptions.

## What this looks like in practice

### A real example: savings apps and virtual accounts at scale

In the same Moniepoint post, the Monnify team says that on 30 July 2019, PiggyVest and Cowrywise created about 100,000 virtual accounts for their users in less than three hours, so each customer could fund their account by transfer and have the payment recognised automatically. Paystack, describing its own virtual account product, says that since 2020 it has processed over 170 million transactions for over 60,000 businesses through virtual accounts ([Paystack blog](https://paystack.com/blog/product/paystack-titan)). Both are the providers' own figures, but they show this isn't experimental technology. It's how many Nigerian fintechs have handled transfers for years. What has changed is that the same tools are now within reach of an ordinary SME.

### An illustrative walkthrough: a Lagos furniture shop

*This is an illustrative example, not a client case study.*

A furniture shop in Lagos takes orders on WhatsApp and Instagram. Customers pay by transfer into the main business account, send screenshots, and the owner confirms each one from her phone, often late at night.

With an automated setup:

1. When a customer confirms an order, the rep's order form creates a Pay with Transfer request for the exact amount and sends the customer the account number with a 30-minute window.
2. The customer pays. Within minutes Paystack sends a signed `charge.success` webhook.
3. A no-code workflow checks the signature, finds the order by its reference, marks it paid in the order sheet and sends the customer a WhatsApp confirmation with the expected delivery date.
4. The dispatch team gets a message: order 1187 is paid and cleared to load.
5. Each evening, the owner gets a summary of payments received, orders still unpaid and any exceptions.

The owner stops being the bottleneck, reps stop arguing with customers about screenshots, and the month-end reconciliation becomes a quick check instead of a weekend job.

## Tools and approaches compared

- **Bank app plus a clear "no release until confirmed" rule.** Free, and better than trusting screenshots, but still manual and still dependent on one person.
- **Payment provider checkout or payment links with transfer.** No code. The provider confirms the payment and it shows on your dashboard. You still update orders by hand unless you connect it to something.
- **No-code automation (provider webhooks into Make, Zapier, n8n or similar, connected to your order sheet or CRM).** Good for most growing SMEs. Needs careful setup of signature checks and duplicate handling, and someone to look after it.
- **A custom integration.** Best for high volume, several payment channels, dedicated accounts for hundreds of customers or complex part-payment rules. Costs more upfront and needs a reliable builder.

Whichever route you take, ask whether it can verify webhooks properly, handle the same event twice safely, deal with part-payments the way your business does, and give you a daily list of unmatched payments.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Treating a screenshot or SMS as proof.** It isn't. Only a confirmed record from your bank or payment provider is.
- **Using one account number for everyone.** It makes automatic matching close to impossible.
- **Skipping the webhook signature check.** That turns your payment system into something a fraudster can talk to directly.
- **Ignoring duplicate events.** Retries are normal. Double deliveries are expensive.
- **Creating dedicated accounts without consent.** Paystack's terms require express consent, and Nigeria's data protection law expects you to be clear about how you use customers' personal data. If you're unsure of your obligations, get advice from a qualified data protection professional.
- **Automating before agreeing the rules.** Decide in advance what happens with part-payments, overpayments and payments into the wrong account. Automation just follows the rules you give it. ## Frequently asked questions

### How can I confirm a bank transfer without checking my bank app?

Give the customer an account number tied to their order or to them, such as Paystack's Pay with Transfer or a dedicated virtual account, and let your provider notify your system by webhook when the money arrives. Your system then marks the order paid automatically.

### How do I know if a bank alert is fake?

Don't rely on the alert at all. Banks advise confirming funds through official channels such as your banking app, USSD or internet banking, and checking that your actual balance has changed. With an automated setup, the only thing that releases an order is a confirmed payment from your provider.

### What is a dedicated virtual account?

It's an account number assigned to one customer, so every transfer into it is automatically recorded as coming from that customer. Providers such as Paystack and Flutterwave offer them, usually with conditions such as business registration, customer consent and, in some cases, identity checks.

### What happens if a customer pays the wrong amount?

It depends on the account type. Paystack and Flutterwave both say wrong amounts sent to their temporary transfer accounts are reversed or refunded automatically. With dedicated accounts, part-payments can arrive, so your system needs a rule for them.

### Do I need a developer to automate payment confirmation?

Not always. Many no-code automation tools can receive webhooks and update a spreadsheet or CRM. You'll want technical help to check webhook signatures properly, handle duplicate events and connect your accounting software.
